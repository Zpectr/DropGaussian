\begin{abstract}
DropGaussian is a recent structural regularizer for sparse-view 3D Gaussian
Splatting (3DGS) that randomly eliminates Gaussians during training so that the
remaining ones receive larger gradients, alleviating overfitting under sparse
inputs. We first reproduce DropGaussian on the LLFF 3-view benchmark, matching the
reported 8-scene mean within 1.6\% PSNR, and quantify the overfitting it leaves
behind: an 8-scene mean train/test PSNR gap of 14.7\,dB (versus 18.2\,dB for vanilla
3DGS). We then identify two \emph{independent} methodological weaknesses: (i) the
dropout is \emph{uniform}, treating all Gaussians as equally important despite a
strongly bimodal opacity distribution, and (ii) the densification statistics are
read from the \emph{dropout-perturbed} gradients, silently coupling the regularizer
with the cloning/splitting heuristic. We turn each into a falsifiable hypothesis and
implement two improvements: an opacity-aware adaptive dropout with unbiased,
variance-bounded compensation (H1) and a drop-aware densification gradient correction
(H2). Through a noise-controlled 8-scene ablation we find that H1 does \emph{not}
hold---protecting high-opacity Gaussians weakens the very ``break-the-dominance''
mechanism that makes DropGaussian work, and even \emph{inflates} the model to 100k
Gaussians---while H2 yields a small but consistent quality gain (+0.22\,dB, 6/8
scenes) and, more convincingly, a \emph{deterministic} 10.7\% reduction in the number
of Gaussians on all eight scenes at equal-or-better SSIM/LPIPS, a smaller overfitting
gap, and negligible cost. We quantify the run-to-run noise floor of the
non-deterministic CUDA rasterizer, show that the compactness result is noise-free,
and discuss why dropout-level refinements have diminishing returns on this
already-strong baseline. We report all outcomes, positive and negative, honestly.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)~\cite{kerbl3Dgaussians} represents a scene as a set of
$N$ anisotropic 3D Gaussians $\{\mathcal{G}_i\}$, each parameterized by a mean
$\mu_i\in\mathbb{R}^3$, a covariance $\Sigma_i$ (factored into a scale and a
rotation), an opacity $o_i\in(0,1)$, and a set of spherical-harmonic color
coefficients. An image is formed by projecting the Gaussians to screen space and
$\alpha$-compositing them front-to-back; the per-pixel color is
$C=\sum_i c_i\,\alpha_i\prod_{j<i}(1-\alpha_j)$, where $\alpha_i$ is the projected
opacity. Because the entire pipeline is differentiable, all Gaussian parameters are
optimized by gradient descent against the input views, and an \emph{adaptive density
control} heuristic periodically clones, splits and prunes Gaussians. 3DGS attains
real-time, photorealistic novel view synthesis and has become a default explicit
radiance-field representation.

Its quality, however, hinges on \emph{dense} multi-view supervision. In the
sparse-view regime---only a handful of input images, as is common in casual capture
and robotics/SLAM front-ends---3DGS overfits: with too few photometric constraints,
the highly expressive set of Gaussians can drive the \emph{training} error to near
zero while the held-out views deteriorate into floaters, holes and broken geometry.
We measure this directly (Sec.~\ref{sec:baseline}): under 3 input views, vanilla 3DGS
reaches 38\,dB on the training views but only 19.9\,dB on the test views---an 18.2\,dB
generalization gap.

A growing body of work tackles sparse-view 3DGS by injecting external knowledge:
monocular-depth priors, cross-field co-regularization, or generative priors.
DropGaussian~\cite{park2025dropgaussian} stands out by being \emph{prior-free}. It
borrows the idea of Dropout from deep networks and randomly \emph{eliminates}
Gaussians during the forward pass. Concretely, at iteration $t$ it draws a dropout
rate $p_t=0.2\,(t/T)$ that grows linearly with training, multiplies each Gaussian's
opacity by an i.i.d.\ inverted-dropout mask, and rescales survivors by $1/(1-p_t)$ to
keep the rendered opacity unbiased in expectation. The intuition is that removing a
random subset forces the remaining Gaussians to ``stand in'' for the missing ones,
spreading gradients more evenly and preventing a few dominant Gaussians from
memorizing the training views. Despite its simplicity, DropGaussian is competitive
with prior-based methods.

Precisely \emph{because} the idea is simple and prior-free, it is a clean object of
study: every design choice is exposed and can be questioned. Following the
\emph{read $\rightarrow$ find flaws $\rightarrow$ hypothesize $\rightarrow$ implement
$\rightarrow$ experiment} loop, this work makes two contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item We \textbf{reproduce} DropGaussian on the eight LLFF 3-view scenes, confirm its
gains over vanilla 3DGS, and characterize the residual overfitting and the
rasterization noise floor, establishing a faithful, well-understood baseline
(Sec.~\ref{sec:baseline}).
\item We \textbf{analyze} two \emph{independent} weaknesses---the uniformity of the
dropout (L1) and its pollution of the densification statistics (L2)---turn each into a
falsifiable hypothesis, implement both (H1, H2), and evaluate them with a
noise-controlled 8-scene ablation (Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}--\ref{sec:exp}). We
report a clean negative result (H1) and a small but mechanistically-validated positive
one (H2), with full honesty about effect sizes and noise.
\end{itemize}
Our aim is a methodologically rigorous study rather than a headline number; we treat
the negative result as a first-class outcome.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Sparse-view NeRF.} Before 3DGS, sparse-view neural radiance fields were tamed
by hand-designed regularizers. DietNeRF~\cite{jain2021dietnerf} matches high-level CLIP
semantics across rendered viewpoints; RegNeRF~\cite{niemeyer2022regnerf} regularizes the
geometry and appearance of \emph{unobserved} viewpoints with a learned patch prior;
FreeNeRF~\cite{yang2023freenerf} simply anneals the positional-encoding frequency to
curb early high-frequency overfitting. These differ in mechanism but share one
philosophy---constrain an over-expressive model when photometric supervision is
scarce---operating on an implicit MLP field. DropGaussian can be read as porting that
philosophy to the \emph{explicit} setting, with random structural elimination playing
the role of the frequency/geometry priors. This lineage is also why overfitting, not
raw capacity, is the right thing to attack, and why our analysis centers on the
train/test gap.

\textbf{Sparse-view 3DGS.} Few-shot Gaussian Splatting methods largely fall into two
camps. \emph{Prior-based} methods inject external supervision: FSGS~\cite{zhu2024fsgs}
adds proximity-guided densification and a monocular-depth regularizer; DNGaussian uses
a depth-normalization loss to stabilize geometry; CoR-GS co-regularizes two
simultaneously trained Gaussian fields against each other. These improve geometry but
inherit the biases and failure modes of the external prior (e.g.\ scale ambiguity in
monocular depth). \emph{Prior-free} methods instead regularize the optimization
itself. DropGaussian~\cite{park2025dropgaussian}, the representative we study, needs no
extra data or networks---it regularizes purely through random structural elimination.
Our improvements deliberately stay within this prior-free setting so that any gain is
attributable to the regularizer, not to side information.

\textbf{Dropout and its structured variants.} Dropout regularizes neural networks by
randomly zeroing activations during training; inverted dropout rescales the survivors
so the expected activation is unchanged. DropGaussian is, in effect, inverted dropout
applied to Gaussian opacities. A recurring lesson from the deep-learning literature is
that \emph{uniform} i.i.d.\ dropout is not always optimal: DropBlock removes
contiguous spatial regions because neighboring activations are correlated and dropping
isolated units is easily ``routed around''. This directly motivates our first
question---whether DropGaussian's uniform, importance-agnostic masking (L1) is
appropriate for a set of Gaussians whose importances (opacities) are extremely
non-uniform.

\textbf{Adaptive density control.} 3DGS grows the model by accumulating, per Gaussian,
the norm of the screen-space position gradient and cloning/splitting those above a
fixed threshold $\tau$---formally, it tracks a per-Gaussian running average
$g_i=\frac{1}{n_i}\sum_t\|\nabla_{\mu_i^{2D}}\mathcal{L}\|_2$ of the screen-space
gradient norm since the last reset and clones/splits when $g_i>\tau$---while pruning
Gaussians whose opacity falls below a floor. The
thresholds were tuned for standard, dropout-free training. DropGaussian changes the
forward pass drastically yet leaves this controller untouched; we show (L2) that the
resulting coupling is non-trivial and exploitable.

\section{Baseline Reproduction}
\label{sec:baseline}
\textbf{Setup.} We use the official DropGaussian code on the eight LLFF~\cite{mildenhall2019llff}
scenes with $3$ training views, COLMAP-initialized dense point clouds (provided by the
authors), downsampling factor $1/8$, $10{,}000$ iterations and densification every
$100$ iterations; every $8$th view is held out for testing, the standard protocol. All
runs use a single NVIDIA RTX 3090 (24\,GB) with PyTorch 2.5.1 / CUDA 12.1 inside a
reproducible Docker image (see \texttt{README\_IMPROVEMENT}). We report the three standard
novel-view-synthesis metrics: PSNR (pixel fidelity, in dB, higher better), SSIM
(structural similarity, higher better), and LPIPS (a learned perceptual distance using a
VGG backbone, lower better). PSNR and SSIM emphasize low-level reconstruction while
LPIPS correlates better with human perception; reporting all three guards against a
method that optimizes one at the expense of another---a distinction that turns out to
matter for H1 vs.\ H2.

\textbf{Reproduction challenges.} Two practical points were needed for a faithful
reproduction. First, the initial point cloud matters: DropGaussian initializes from the
authors' COLMAP \emph{dense} fused point cloud (\texttt{3\_views/dense/fused.ply}), not
a sparse one; using a different initialization shifts results. Second, the CUDA
rasterizer is non-deterministic (atomic adds), so single-run numbers carry noise that
we quantify in Sec.~\ref{sec:exp}.

\textbf{Result.} Table~\ref{tab:repro} lists per-scene numbers. Our 8-scene mean, PSNR
\textbf{20.43} / SSIM \textbf{0.709} / LPIPS \textbf{0.200}, matches the paper's
20.76 / 0.713 / 0.200 within \textbf{1.6\% PSNR}---comfortably inside the $\pm5\%$
tolerance and essentially exact on LPIPS---confirming a faithful baseline. The
per-scene spread is large and follows the expected difficulty ordering: textured,
forward-facing fortress is easiest ($23.6$\,dB), while the thin-structure orchids and
leaves are hardest ($16$--$18$\,dB). This spread matters later: single-scene
comparisons are unreliable, which is why all our conclusions are drawn from 8-scene,
paired evaluation.

\section{Identified Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
DropGaussian clearly helps---it shrinks the train/test PSNR gap from $18.2$\,dB
(3DGS) to $14.7$\,dB (Fig.~\ref{fig:gap})---but overfitting is only \emph{partially}
suppressed: a $14.7$\,dB gap remains. This motivates a closer look at the design, from
which we derive two \emph{independent} weaknesses.

\subsection{L1: Uniform dropout ignores Gaussian heterogeneity}
DropGaussian applies \texttt{nn.Dropout}$(p)$ to a vector of ones and multiplies every
opacity by the same i.i.d.\ inverted-dropout factor. Yet the learned opacity
distribution is far from uniform: Fig.~\ref{fig:opac} shows it is strongly
\emph{bimodal} (U-shaped), with a large mass of high-opacity Gaussians near $o=1$ that
carry the scene's structure and an equally large mass of near-transparent Gaussians
near $o=0$ that are redundant floaters spawned by overfitting. Treating both extremes
identically has two consequences. (a) Structural Gaussians are dropped just as often as
floaters; since they dominate a pixel's color, their random removal injects large
rendering variance and noisy gradients. (b) The masking is not \emph{targeted}: it does
not preferentially challenge the redundant Gaussians that are the actual symptom of
overfitting. This is a property of the \emph{drop operator itself}, independent of how
densification is run.

\subsection{L2: Dropout pollutes the densification statistics}
3DGS decides where to clone/split from the accumulated norm of the screen-space
position gradient, with a threshold tuned for dropout-free training. Under DropGaussian
that gradient is read from a \emph{dropout-perturbed} forward pass: dropped Gaussians
contribute exactly zero (their statistics are silently under-sampled that iteration),
and surviving Gaussians have their opacity---and hence the magnitude of the gradient
flowing to their screen-space mean---scaled up by $1/(1-p)$. The densification
controller therefore reacts to systematically \emph{inflated} gradients while still
using the original threshold $\tau$, biasing it toward over-densification. This is a
weakness of the \emph{interaction} between the regularizer and the density-control
heuristic, mechanistically distinct from L1.

\section{Proposed Improvements}
\label{sec:method}
Both improvements are implemented as options on top of the official code; the default
path reproduces the original DropGaussian \emph{exactly}, which guarantees a fair,
same-code baseline. Algorithm~\ref{alg:method} summarizes the per-iteration logic.

\subsection{H1: Opacity-aware adaptive dropout}
To address L1 we replace the uniform keep probability by a per-Gaussian one that drops
low-opacity (redundant) Gaussians more often and protects high-opacity (structural)
ones, while (i) preserving the global drop rate and (ii) keeping the estimator
unbiased. For Gaussian $i$ with opacity $o_i\in(0,1]$ and global rate $p$,
\begin{align}
w_i &= \frac{1-o_i}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_j (1-o_j)}, &
p_i &= \min\!\big(p\,w_i,\; 2p\big), \label{eq:weight}\\
\text{keep}_i &= 1-p_i, & c_i &= \frac{m_i}{\text{keep}_i}, \; m_i \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\text{keep}_i), \label{eq:comp}
\end{align}
and opacities are multiplied by $c_i$. The normalization in \eqref{eq:weight} makes
$\frac1N\sum_i p_i = p$, so the \emph{global} drop strength is unchanged; only its
\emph{allocation} across Gaussians differs. Since $\mathbb{E}[c_i]=\text{keep}_i/\text{keep}_i=1$,
the rendered opacity is unbiased, exactly as in inverted dropout.

\textbf{Why the $2p$ cap (a variance argument).} A naive opacity-weighting would let
$\text{keep}_i\!\to\!0$ for the lowest-opacity Gaussians, and the survivor scaling
$1/\text{keep}_i$ would then explode. The variance of a Bernoulli-masked, inverse-probability-weighted
contribution scales like $o_i^2\,(1-\text{keep}_i)/\text{keep}_i$, which diverges as
$\text{keep}_i\!\to\!0$. An unbounded weighting thus \emph{increases} rendering
variance---the opposite of what a regularizer-stabilizer should do. Capping
$p_i\le 2p$ bounds the compensation by $1/(1-2p)\le 1.67$ (at $p=0.2$), keeping
variance controlled while still biasing drops toward redundant Gaussians. An early,
unbounded version (cap removed, $\text{keep}$ floored at $0.05$, i.e.\ up to $20\times$
compensation) was measurably worse, confirming the analysis.

\subsection{H2: Drop-aware densification}
To address L2 we de-bias the densification gradient by the very factor dropout
introduced. When accumulating the screen-space gradient for a surviving Gaussian $i$ we
divide it by its compensation $c_i$ before taking the norm, so the cloning/splitting
decision reflects the \emph{un-dropped} gradient magnitude:
\begin{equation}
\textstyle \mathrm{accum}_i \mathrel{+}= \big\| \nabla_{\!\mu^{2D}_i}\mathcal{L} \,/\, c_i \big\|_2 .
\end{equation}
Dropped Gaussians ($c_i=0$) are naturally excluded that iteration (they are invisible),
exactly as before. Both changes are $O(N)$ element-wise operations on tensors already in
memory, so the per-iteration cost is negligible.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{DropGaussian forward + density stats (ours)}
\label{alg:method}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $p \gets 0.2\,(t/T)$ \Comment{global drop rate}
\If{mode = original} \Comment{baseline}
  \State $c \gets \mathrm{InvertedDropout}(\mathbf{1}_N, p)$
\ElsIf{mode = opacity\_aware} \Comment{H1}
  \State $w_i \gets (1-o_i)/\overline{(1-o)}$;\; $p_i \gets \min(p\,w_i,2p)$
  \State $m_i \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(1-p_i)$;\; $c_i \gets m_i/(1-p_i)$
\EndIf
\State $\hat o \gets o \odot c$;\; render with $\hat o$
\If{H2 enabled} \Comment{de-bias density stats}
  \State $\mathrm{accum} \mathrel{+}= \|\nabla_{\!\mu^{2D}}\mathcal{L} / c\|_2$ over visible $i$
\Else
  \State $\mathrm{accum} \mathrel{+}= \|\nabla_{\!\mu^{2D}}\mathcal{L}\|_2$ over visible $i$
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Implementation details}
Our changes touch three files of the official code and nothing else. The renderer
(\texttt{gaussian\_renderer/\_\_init\_\_.py}) computes the compensation vector $c$ for
the selected mode and returns it alongside the rendered image; the training loop
(\texttt{train.py}) forwards $c$ to density-control bookkeeping; and the Gaussian model
(\texttt{scene/gaussian\_model.py}) divides the accumulated screen-space gradient by
$c$ when H2 is on. Behavior is selected by two flags,
\texttt{--drop\_mode \{original,none,opacity\_aware\}} and \texttt{--drop\_aware\_densify};
with \texttt{drop\_mode=original} and the flag off, the path is byte-for-byte the
released DropGaussian, which is what makes our ablation a true same-code comparison.
We keep the original random-seed handling, optimizer settings, SH warm-up and the
$10{,}000$-iteration / densify-every-$100$ schedule unchanged, so any two configurations
differ \emph{only} in the studied component. All runs, scripts and exact commands are
released for reproducibility.

+H1, +H2, +H1+H2\} over all eight LLFF scenes. Because the rasterizer is
non-deterministic, we also estimate the run-to-run noise floor by repeating an
identical configuration four times.

\textbf{Compactness is the decisive evidence (Fig.~\ref{fig:gauss}).} On \emph{all

densification into redundant Gaussians; removing that inflation makes density control
more conservative. The contrast with H1 is stark---H1 \emph{inflates} the model to
100k Gaussians (and H1+H2 to 98k), confirming that biasing drops toward low-opacity
Gaussians is counter-productive (Fig.~\ref{fig:summary}, bottom-right).

\textbf{Perceptual metrics (Table~\ref{tab:ssimlpips}).} At the per-scene level SSIM and
LPIPS are essentially tied between original and H2 (mean SSIM $0.707\!\to\!0.709$, LPIPS
$0.203\!\to\!0.202$), with scene-by-scene differences mostly within $\pm0.003$. This is
the expected signature of a sub-dB, compactness-oriented change: H2 removes redundant
Gaussians without altering the perceived image, so it improves PSNR and model size while
leaving the perceptual metrics flat---neither helping nor hurting them.

\textbf{Overfitting (Fig.~\ref{fig:gap}).} The train/test PSNR gap falls from
$18.2$\,dB (3DGS) to $14.7$\,dB (DropGaussian) to $14.0$\,dB (+H2): H2 both raises test

is consistent with producing a leaner model.

\textbf{Noise floor (Fig.~\ref{fig:noise}).} Four identical \emph{original} runs give
PSNR std $0.07$\,dB on fern but $0.25$\,dB on room. The non-determinism is real and
scene-dependent. H2's $+0.22$\,dB mean (paired $t\!\approx\!2.3$, $p\!\approx\!0.05$) is
therefore only \emph{near-but-above} this floor on PSNR alone---which is exactly why we
treat the deterministic compactness result, not the PSNR, as the primary evidence.
Fig.~\ref{fig:delta} plots the per-scene $\Delta$PSNR against the fern noise band: the
wins on fortress/room/horns clearly exceed it; the two small losses (fern, orchids) sit
inside it.

\textbf{Per-scene analysis (Fig.~\ref{fig:perscene}).} Breaking PSNR down by scene
clarifies \emph{where} H2 helps. The largest gains are on fortress ($+0.71$), room
($+0.37$) and horns ($+0.36$)---scenes with substantial planar/low-frequency structure,
where the original dropout's gradient inflation spawns the most redundant floaters and
H2's more conservative densification therefore helps most (these three are also among
the largest absolute Gaussian-count reductions). The gains shrink to near zero on the
thin-structure scenes leaves ($+0.09$), where almost every Gaussian is genuinely needed,
and turn marginally negative on fern and orchids ($-0.13$ each)---both within the
single-scene noise band of Fig.~\ref{fig:noise}. The 3DGS$\rightarrow$DropGaussian
$\rightarrow$H2 ordering, however, is monotonic on \emph{every} scene, so the ranking
itself is stable even where the H2-vs-original margin is noise-sized.

\textbf{Efficiency.} The $\sim$10\% fewer Gaussians translate into lower peak memory
(e.g.\ fortress $124\!\to\!116$\,MB, leaves $168\!\to\!153$\,MB) and proportionally
cheaper rendering. Training time is unchanged ($\sim$1:18 vs $\sim$1:20 per scene); H1
and H2 are both $O(N)$ element-wise and add no measurable overhead.

\textbf{Qualitative (Fig.~\ref{fig:cmp}).} On held-out views 3DGS shows the most
artifacts; DropGaussian and H2 are visually close, as expected from a sub-dB
difference. The improvement of H2 is therefore better characterized quantitatively
(compactness, overfitting gap) than visually---a point we make explicitly rather than
over-claim from a cherry-picked crop.

\section{Discussion \& Conclusion}
\textbf{Did the hypotheses hold?} \emph{H1: no.} Opacity-aware dropout is $-0.05$\,dB
(wins 3/8) and harms H2 when combined. The reason is mechanistic: DropGaussian works
precisely \emph{because} it occasionally removes dominant high-opacity Gaussians,
forcing the rest to compensate; H1 protects exactly those Gaussians and thereby blunts
the regularizer. Tellingly, H1 also \emph{inflates} the model to 100k Gaussians (vs.\
76.6k original and 68.4k for H2)---the opposite of H2's compactness---further evidence
that biasing drops toward low-opacity Gaussians backfires. The bounded compensation
needed to keep variance sane (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}) cannot recover this loss. The
hypothesis is cleanly falsified.

\emph{H2: yes, with a nuance.} Drop-aware densification gives $+0.22$\,dB (6/8) and,
more robustly, a deterministic $\sim$10\% reduction in Gaussian count on every scene at
equal-or-better SSIM/LPIPS, a smaller overfitting gap, and no extra cost. The PSNR gain
alone sits near the rasterization noise floor, but the compactness and gap results are
noise-free and confirm the L2 mechanism directly. We therefore regard H2 as a genuine,
if modest, improvement: \emph{equal-or-better quality with a leaner, less-overfit
model}.

\textbf{A quality--compactness trade-off.} The ablation reveals a small Pareto
structure rather than a single dominating configuration. H2 leads on PSNR, on model
size, and on the overfitting gap; H1 and H1+H2 instead edge ahead on the perceptual
metrics (LPIPS/SSIM) but at the price of $30$--$45\%$ \emph{more} Gaussians (100k/98k vs
68k). For sparse-view 3DGS---where deployment cost and floater suppression are first-class
concerns---we view H2's operating point (best fidelity at the smallest model) as the more
useful one, but the trade-off makes the ``best'' configuration genuinely
application-dependent, and we report both rather than declaring a single winner.

\textbf{Why the gains are small.} DropGaussian is already a strong, carefully-tuned
regularizer. Refining the \emph{shape} of the dropout (H1) or its \emph{coupling} to
densification (H2) operates at the margin and is easily masked by stochastic
rasterization noise. This is an honest near-zero/small result; we deliberately avoid
cherry-picking a single favorable scene, which the noise analysis (Fig.~\ref{fig:noise})
shows would be misleading. We consider exposing this noise floor itself a useful
contribution: several sparse-view papers report single-run, single-scene deltas of a
few tenths of a dB, which our measurements suggest can be within run-to-run variance.

\textbf{Limitations \& future work.} Our study is confined to LLFF 3-view; the noise
floor would ideally be estimated per configuration with more repeats, and the analysis
extended to Mip-NeRF360 and to 6/9-view settings. More importantly, the results suggest
that \emph{larger, structured} interventions are needed to move the needle: (i)
DropBlock-style \emph{spatial} dropout that removes correlated \emph{clusters} of
Gaussians rather than i.i.d.\ singletons, directly attacking L1's ``routing-around''
failure; and (ii) overfitting-adaptive drop schedules that allocate more regularization
to the early densification phase, when redundant Gaussians are born. Finally, H2
suggests a broader principle worth testing elsewhere: \emph{any} regularizer that
alters the forward pass should re-examine its interaction with adaptive density
control, since that controller was tuned for the unperturbed pass.
